## **Abstract Reframe (250 words)**

> Financial institutions increasingly delegate complaint resolution to large language models, affecting millions of consumers' economic wellbeing. This delegation raises fundamental questions about corporate responsibility: when organizations deploy AI systems that discriminate, who bears ethical accountability?
>
> We present evidence from 44,154 controlled experiments across four commercial LLMs revealing two ethically troubling patterns. First, models exhibit "stereotype assumption bias"—reducing information-gathering by 80% when demographic markers are present, effectively prejudging customer needs based on identity. Second, discrimination quadruples in high-severity cases involving foreclosure or bankruptcy risk, creating maximum harm precisely when vulnerable stakeholders need fair treatment most.
>
> Critically, we find that vendor selection constitutes an ethical choice with measurable consequences: GPT-4o exhibits minimal bias (0.058) while GPT-4o-mini shows 2.4× more discrimination despite coming from the same provider. No universal mitigation strategy exists—what works for one model fails for another. These findings challenge the assumption that AI deployment transfers ethical responsibility to technology vendors.
>
> We argue that corporations deploying AI for consequential consumer decisions bear irreducible ethical obligations: to conduct severity-stratified auditing, monitor procedural fairness (not just outcomes), and select vendors based on empirical fairness testing rather than cost or capability alone. The current practice of treating AI as a neutral efficiency tool, rather than an ethical agent acting on the corporation's behalf, fails the stakeholders most dependent on fair treatment.

## **Introduction Framing**

### Opening Hook (Business Ethics Angle)

> "When Bank of America or Wells Fargo deploys an AI system to handle customer complaints, they make an implicit promise: that customers will receive fair consideration regardless of who they are. Our research reveals this promise is systematically broken—not through human prejudice, but through algorithmic systems that discriminate most severely against the most vulnerable customers in their most desperate moments."

### The Ethical Problem (Not Technical Problem)

Frame around  **three business ethics tensions** :

1. **The Delegation Paradox** : Organizations cannot delegate ethical responsibility by delegating decisions to algorithms. When AI discriminates, the deploying corporation—not the AI vendor—bears primary ethical accountability.
2. **The Efficiency-Fairness Tradeoff** : Cost pressures drive adoption of smaller, cheaper models (GPT-4o-mini) that exhibit 2.4× more discrimination than premium alternatives. Is cost-cutting that harms vulnerable customers ethically permissible?
3. **The Audit Illusion** : Current fairness evaluations miss severity-dependent bias. Organizations believing their AI is "fair" based on aggregate metrics are engaging in unintentional ethics washing.

## **Theoretical Framing Options**

### Option A: Stakeholder Theory

Frame discrimination as harm to vulnerable stakeholder groups. The 4× bias amplification in high-severity cases represents abandonment of stakeholders precisely when fiduciary duty is most acute.

 **Key citations** : Freeman (1984), Mitchell et al. (1997) on stakeholder salience, Greenwood & Van Buren (2010) on stakeholder trust.

### Option B: Corporate Moral Agency

Argue that AI systems act as moral agents on behalf of corporations. When they discriminate, they express corporate values. Vendor selection is therefore an expression of corporate ethical commitments.

 **Key citations** : French (1979), Velasquez (2003), Arnold (2006) on corporate moral agency.

### Option C: Procedural Justice

Your "stereotype assumption bias" (80% reduction in questioning) is fundamentally about procedural unfairness—not giving stakeholders voice before making decisions affecting them.

 **Key citations** : Leventhal (1980), Colquitt (2001), Cropanzano et al. (2007) on organizational justice.

### Option D: Ethics of Care

Frame high-severity bias as failure of care ethics—the 4× discrimination amplification means systems fail most when stakeholders are most vulnerable and most in need of careful, attentive treatment.

 **Key citations** : Held (2006), Hamington (2019) on care ethics in business.
