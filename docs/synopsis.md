
## Synopsis

**Problem:** As organizations deploy large language models (LLMs) to handle customer complaints, a critical question emerges: do these models treat customers differently based on how they write, rather than what they complain about? Prior research on linguistic bias in AI has largely focused on African-American English and race-based discrimination. I expand this lens to examine whether writing style triggers inferences about age, gender, geography, socio-economic status, and education—and whether those inferences lead to different remedies for identical complaints.

**Method:** I conducted a controlled audit of two commercial LLMs using approximately 5,000 real financial services complaints. I defined 36 persona styles (varying along six demographic dimensions) and rewrote complaints to match these styles while holding the underlying facts constant. Using a downsampled design, I generated 18,000 matched decisions per model. I measured three things: (1) whether models infer customer demographics from writing style alone, (2) whether those inferences predict different remedy recommendations, and (3) whether a practical intervention—rewriting complaints into neutral language before decision-making—reduces disparate treatment.

**Findings:** Both models confidently infer customer demographics from complaint text, though they construct opposite "default customers" (one model assumes young/urban/female; the other assumes older/suburban/male). Holding complaint content constant, one model shows outcome gaps of 8–20 percentage points across demographic dimensions (e.g., 69% favorable outcomes for female-coded writing vs. 49% for male-coded); the other model shows no meaningful style-based disparities. Style neutralization eliminates measurable outcome disparities for the second model but fails to reduce them for the first. I also test whether bias amplifies for higher-stakes complaints, finding directional but inconclusive evidence.

**Contribution:** This study provides the first end-to-end audit tracing the full causal chain—from linguistic style to inferred persona to remedy severity—in a realistic consumer complaint pipeline. By expanding beyond race to infer multiple demographic characteristics, I demonstrate that linguistic bias affects a broader population than previously documented. The research also tests a practical mitigation strategy on proprietary models and measures its effectiveness.
